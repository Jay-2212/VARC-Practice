Theory and rules

An inference is not stated, and must be true.

Definition

An inference is a statement that is not explicitly stated in the passage but that must be true if everything the passage says is true.

Must be true: not "probably", not "likely given human nature", not "what the author would want". CAT is sometimes lenient: a close paraphrase can be the answer, so don't reject an option because it is nearly stated.

The must-be-true test

"Is it possible for everything in the passage to be true and this option to be false?"

  • Yes, possible → not an inference. Eliminate.
  • No, impossible → valid inference.

Rule 2.1

The correct inference usually sits one short step from the text.

Text says X. Therefore Y: one step. Therefore probably Z: two steps, danger. If you need an "and so probably…", you are extrapolating.

Rule 2.2

Prove the right answer; don't just fail to disprove it.

Point to the line. If you cannot, you are choosing on vibes.

Valid inferences CAT likes

Theory and rules

Evidence, a gap, a conclusion. The assumption fills the gap.

Definition

An assumption is an unstated premise that must be true for the argument's conclusion to follow from its evidence.

It is about the argument, not the world. Whether it is true in real life doesn't matter; whether the argument needs it does.

Evidence

Facts, data, observations, examples the author gives. Stated.

The gap

Whenever the conclusion has a new term or a new relationship not in the evidence, there is a gap. The assumption bridges it.

Conclusion

What the author wants you to accept. Signals: therefore, thus, hence, so, consequently, it follows that, we must, should, clearly, this shows.

The "new term" method. Write evidence and conclusion as two short lines; underline any term in the conclusion that isn't in the evidence. The assumption connects old term to new.

Evidence: students who attend the optional evening classes score higher. Conclusion: the classes improve performance. New element: causation. Assumption: the higher scores are not explained by some other difference, such as more motivated students choosing to attend.

The negation test, your scalpel

Negate the option. If the negated version destroys the argument, it is an assumption. If the argument survives, it is not.

An overreaching option is stronger than necessary. Negate a strong statement and you get a weak one, which usually doesn't destroy the argument. An assumption is the minimum the argument needs, not the maximum that would help it.

"All X are Y" → "No X are Y" is the opposite, not the negation, and it makes extreme options look like assumptions.

What an assumption is not

  • The conclusion restated. It sounds central; it is stated.
  • A piece of evidence restated. If it's in the passage, it isn't assumed.
  • An inference. An inference follows from the text; an assumption is underneath it.
  • Something the author would merely agree with.
  • Stronger than needed.

Necessary vs sufficient

"Assumption", "presupposes", "relies on", "depends on" mean necessary: use the negation test. "Which, if assumed, would allow the conclusion to be properly drawn?" means sufficient; rare in CAT, and strong options can win there.

The "defender" assumption

"The improvement was not due to [alternative cause]." Dull, negative, often correct: it patches the gap's weakest point.

Theory and rules

Topic, scope, main idea and purpose are four different questions.

Wrong options love to answer the topic when you were asked for the main idea.

TermQuestion it answersExample
TopicWhat is it about, in 1–3 words?Urban streets
ScopeWhich specific aspect of the topic?How 20th-century planners valued streets
Main idea Central claimWhat does the author assert about the scope?Planners erred by valuing only measurable movement
Primary purposeWhat is the author doing? (a verb)To critique a planning approach and identify its core flaw

"The passage is about urban streets": true, useless. A main idea must contain a claim, usually with the author's stance.

The scope ladder

▲ Broader

  1. Too broad"The history of cities" / "Urban life in general"
  2. Too broad"Approaches to urban planning"
  3. Just right"Planners' exclusive focus on measurable flow was a mistake"Covers every paragraph, carries the stance
  4. Too narrow"Wider roads caused congestion to return"One paragraph
  5. Too narrow"Street vendors were removed to improve flow"One detail

▼ Narrower

Too narrow

The most common "closer but not the answer" trap.

Captures one paragraph or one example. It's correct, it's from the passage, it's just a slice.

  • Fingerprint: a specific example, name, number or case from a single paragraph
  • Test: "Does this cover paragraph 1 to the last?"

Too broad

Covers far more than the passage discusses.

  • Fingerprint: "in general", "throughout history", "all forms of", "society", "humanity"
  • Test: "Would the author need a whole book, not 400 words, to support this?"

Just right

  • Every paragraph contributes to it
  • Includes the author's stance: critique, defence, qualification, proposal

Theory and rules

Cohesion first, because you can mark it with a pen.

Cohesion

The grammatical and lexical glue between sentences: pronouns that point back, articles that assume prior mention, connectors, repeated or echoed words. Visible on the surface. Objective.

Coherence

The logical flow of ideas: general → specific, claim → evidence, problem → solution, cause → effect, chronology. It lives in the meaning. Interpretive, so it comes second.

Tool 2 · a/an vs the

A/an introduces something new; the refers to something already introduced. "A stepwell" in X and "the stepwell" in Y: X comes first.

"The" also marks unique or known things (the sun, the Mughal empire, the twentieth century). Use the signal only for nouns that would need an introduction.

Tool 4 · Time sequence

Dates, eras, "initially", "later", "by the 1990s", "since then". "Now" or "today" often comes late (the present after the history); "for centuries" or "long" often comes early.

Tool 5 · General → specific

General claim → narrower claim → example or evidence → implication → (broader conclusion). Paragraphs narrow, then may widen again at the end.

Tool 6 · Echo words

  • "the pressure cooker" → "the appliance" (category)
  • "promised speed… and fuel saved" → "both promises" (summary)
  • "active molecular processes" → "Such a mechanism" (category)

A summary echo ("both promises", "these factors") needs all its items before it.

Tool 7 · Acronyms and names

Full form before acronym: "the Reserve Bank of India (RBI)" before "the RBI". Full name before surname. "A young economist named X" before "X argued".

Theory and rules

A summary restates the central claim, with its qualifier, in the author's stance, and adds nothing.

Five requirements

  • Core claim: the main point the author is making
  • Essential qualifier: any condition, limit or "but" that changes the meaning ("not X but Y", "only under Z")
  • Correct stance: the author's view, not one the author reports and rejects
  • No new information: no new cause, prediction or prescription
  • Proportionate: emphasises what the paragraph emphasises; an example is not the main point

A summary is not

  • The first sentence (often the setup, or the view being challenged)
  • The most vivid example
  • A list of everything mentioned
  • A conclusion beyond the paragraph

Theory and rules · Odd one out

One thread, one intruder: same topic, different angle.

A coherent paragraph has a single thread: a topic and an angle. CAT examiners rarely add an off-topic sentence. They add an on-topic, off-angle one, which is what makes it tricky.

How the odd sentence differs

  • Different angle on the same topic forecasts' use to farmers vs aerosol modelling
  • Different time frame present vs origins

C · Tenses

Core logic

  • Simple past: completed, at a specific time. "She joined in 2019."
  • Present perfect: relevant to now, no specific time. "She has worked here since 2019." ✗ "She has joined in 2019."
  • Past perfect: the earlier of two past actions. "By the time the results came out, he had already accepted the offer."

Misplaced modifier

A modifier sits next to what it modifies.

An introductory phrase must describe the subject of the main clause.

E · Parallelism

Items in a list, comparison or paired structure take the same grammatical form.

Theory and rules · RC selection

The 60-second scan: choose passages by their questions, not their topics.

01

First two lines

Is the topic familiar, or at least imaginable?

Familiarity helps speed, not accuracy. Still useful.

02

Density

Long, nested sentences with abstract nouns are slow

"The ontological commitments of hermeneutic…" goes last.

03

Question stems

Count direct vs hard stems

  • Direct detail, function with line reference, main idea
  • Hard EXCEPT across the passage, analogy, least agree

04

Length

Shorter isn't always easier, but very long + abstract = last

Rank by question friendliness × readability, and attempt in that order.

Rule 12.1

A dull passage on soil chemistry with four direct questions beats a fascinating one on philosophy with two EXCEPT-inferences and an analogy.

How many passages

Plan for 3 full passages plus selective questions from the 4th: 12–14 RC attempts. Never skip a passage in frustration; skip it by decision after the scan, or take only its 1–2 friendliest questions.

Reading pace

About 250–300 words a minute on CAT-density text, building the map as you go. A 500-word passage: ~2 minutes to read and map; ~1–1.3 minutes per question.

Read fast without losing the thread

  • Read for structure: first and last sentence of each paragraph in full, the middle for gist
  • Mark pivots: however, but, yet, in fact
  • Note whose voice: author or reported view
  • No re-reading on the first pass; mark an opaque sentence and move on
  • Map immediately: 5–8 words per paragraph

Speed gym to Nov 29

One editorial or long-form essay daily (Aeon, The Hindu op-eds, Mint long reads) with a 2-line map: 10 minutes. In mocks, time each passage read; consistently over 3 minutes for 500 words means you're re-reading.

Theory and rules · When to drop

Drop questions by rule. Drop passages only by decision.

Drop a question when

  • You've spent 2 minutes and can't name the zone in the passage
  • You're re-reading the same paragraph a third time
  • Your heart rate is up and you're arguing with the options rather than checking the text

Drop a passage when

Only after the scan, or after taking its easy questions:

  • The first read took over 4 minutes and the map is still unclear
  • 3 of 4 questions are EXCEPT / analogy / least-likely and you're short of time

Never

A hard first question is not information about the next three.

Never drop a passage because one question went badly. Take the main-idea question: after a full read it's often the easiest.

Theory and rules · Calm

The crash is a spiral. The fix is procedural.

Stuck → anxiety → skip a whole passage → rush the rest → errors.

01

The 2-minute rule

A rule, not a guideline

When a question's timer passes 2 minutes, you mark and move. No negotiation.

02

Reset breath

Between passages: 4 seconds in, 6 out

Sounds trivial. It resets the stuck feeling.

03

Expect it

Every CAT has one horrible passage

When it arrives, say "there it is", scan its questions for the 1–2 friendly ones, take those, and leave.

04

No mid-section maths

Don't calculate your score mid-section

It's always worse in your head than on the scorecard.

05

Accuracy

Your aim is accuracy, not completion

Completion is a trap that turns good attempts into bad ones.

Worked example 2 · Triage within a passage Reservation for small industry

Passage

In the decades after independence, India's planners treated small-scale industry as a source of employment to be protected rather than a sector to be grown. Hundreds of products — from pickles to padlocks — were "reserved" for small firms, which meant that larger companies could not manufacture them. The policy's defenders argued that it would spread industrial jobs beyond a few cities and prevent large firms from crushing artisans.

Critics, by the 1990s, had assembled a different picture. Reservation, they argued, had trapped firms at small sizes: a firm that grew beyond the limit lost its protection, so many chose to stay small or to split into several nominally separate units. In industries such as garments and toys, where scale brings lower costs and the ability to fill large export orders, Indian producers lost ground to competitors in countries without such limits.

The reservation list was steadily pruned after 1997 and abolished in 2015. Whether its removal produced the growth critics predicted remains debated; firm size depends on much else, from labour laws to access to credit.

S1 · An EXCEPT opens RC3

22 minutes into the section, you've done 6 VA and 2 RCs (8 questions). You open RC3 and the first question is an EXCEPT across the whole passage. What do you do?

Rao's answer

Don't start with the EXCEPT. Scan the other three stems for a main-idea or line-referenced question and answer those first. Return to the EXCEPT with T/F/CBD if you have 2 minutes or more left in your RC3 budget. You're at 8 questions with 18 minutes left, on track for 15–17 attempts, so stay calm.

S2 · A PJ at 2.5 minutes

On a 5-sentence PJ, after 2.5 minutes you have the opener and one lock (3-1) but can't place sentences 2, 4 and 5. What do you do?

Rao's answer

Spend at most 30 more seconds testing the likeliest position for the rest using general → specific. Then enter your best order (TITA has no negative marking) and move on. Mark for review only if you finish with time.

S3 · B or D at 1:40

You've eliminated two options with named traps and are left with B and D. You lean D but can't find the unsupported word in B. 1 minute 40 seconds have passed. What do you do?

Rao's answer

Go back to the text for 20 seconds, not the options, and reread the zone. Still undecided: mark D, your lean (a true 50–50 is worth +1), and flag it for pass 2. Don't go past about 2 minutes on it now.
